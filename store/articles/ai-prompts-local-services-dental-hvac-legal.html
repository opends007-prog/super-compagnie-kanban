AI Playbooks for Local Services: 60 Ready-to-Paste Prompts for Dental, HVAC & Legal Offices

Most local service businesses don't have an AI problem. They have a blank-page problem. The owner signs up for ChatGPT or Claude, stares at the cursor, types "write me a social media post," gets mush, and concludes the technology is overhyped.

The fix is not a better model. It's a better prompt. A prompt that names your trade, your customer, your constraints, and the exact format you want back will outperform a vague request every single time. This article gives you the framework behind good prompts, plus a working set you can copy, adapt, and use this week — organized for dental practices, HVAC companies, and law offices, with the compliance traps each trade needs to respect.

The anatomy of a prompt that works

Every strong business prompt has five parts:

  1. Role. "You are a patient coordinator at a family dental practice in suburban Ohio."
  2. Context. Two or three sentences about the situation — the customer, the season, the constraint.
  3. Task. One verb. "Draft," "rewrite," "summarize," "list." Not three verbs.
  4. Format. Length, tone, structure. "Under 120 words, warm but not salesy, no exclamation points."
  5. Guardrails. What it must not do. "Do not mention prices. Do not diagnose. Do not promise outcomes."

Miss one and quality drops. Include all five and even a mid-tier model produces usable first drafts.

Dental: prompts that fill chairs without crossing lines

Dental marketing lives under two constraints: patient privacy (HIPAA) and state dental board advertising rules. Never paste patient information into a public AI tool. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services is unambiguous that health information must be de-identified before use outside covered systems [1]. Prompts below assume zero patient data.

Recall and reactivation

You are the front-desk lead at a family dental practice. Write a 90-word reactivation email to patients who haven't booked a cleaning in 9–14 months. Tone: friendly, zero guilt. Include one sentence on why regular cleanings matter, a soft call to action, and a placeholder [BOOKING LINK]. No discounts, no urgency tactics.

Review responses

Draft a reply to this 4-star Google review: "[paste review with all identifying details removed]". Rules: thank the reviewer, never confirm or deny they are a patient, never reference any treatment, keep it under 60 words, invite them to call the office directly.

That "never confirm they are a patient" line matters — even acknowledging someone is a patient in a public reply can create HIPAA exposure.

Treatment-plan explanation (for staff use)

Explain what a dental crown procedure involves, in plain language a nervous patient could understand, in under 150 words. Do not give medical advice, do not quote costs, and end with "your dentist will walk you through the specifics."

Print it, hand it to patients, cut the front-desk explanation time in half.

HVAC: prompts built around seasons and urgency

HVAC demand is spiky — heat waves and cold snaps drive the phones. Your prompts should be ready before the weather turns.

Seasonal tune-up campaign

You are the marketing lead for a residential HVAC company in [city]. Write three versions of a spring AC tune-up email, each under 100 words: one for homeowners with systems over 10 years old, one for new homeowners, one for last year's customers. Practical tone, one call to action each, no scare tactics about breakdowns.

After-hours call script

Write a 60-second phone script for an answering service taking emergency HVAC calls. It must capture: name, address, system type if known, symptoms, and whether anyone in the home is vulnerable to heat or cold. Calm, competent tone. End with the expected callback window: [YOUR WINDOW].

Technician leave-behind

Write a one-page "How to make your furnace last longer" sheet a tech can leave after a service call. Five tips, plain language, no brand names, no upsell language. Grade 6 reading level.

Review generation (the honest way)

Write a short text message a technician can send after a completed job asking the customer to leave an honest Google review. Under 40 words. No incentives mentioned, no pressure, link placeholder included.

Note the word "honest." The FTC's rule on consumer reviews bans fake and incentivized-without-disclosure reviews, with civil penalties per violation [2]. Never ask AI to write reviews for customers — only to ask customers to write their own.

Legal: prompts with a confidentiality firewall

Lawyers have the strictest constraints of the three. ABA Model Rule 1.1 (competence) includes understanding the technology you use, and Rule 1.6 (confidentiality) governs what you can disclose [3]. Several state bars have issued guidance that generative AI use is permissible with safeguards — but client-identifying information should never go into a consumer AI tool without the provider's confidentiality terms reviewed first.

Safe territory: marketing, internal process, and generic educational content.

Practice-area page draft

You are a content writer for a small estate-planning firm in [state]. Draft a 400-word FAQ section answering: "Do I need a will if I don't own much?" Write for a worried 55-year-old reader. Plain English, no legalese. End every answer with "this is general information, not legal advice — consult an attorney about your situation."

Intake form design

List the 12 questions a family-law intake form should ask before a first consultation, ordered from least to most sensitive. Flag any question where the answer might be privileged and note that the form should say responses are confidential.

Client communication polish

Rewrite this email to a client to be clearer and calmer, without changing any facts or legal positions: "[paste your own draft with client identifiers removed]". Keep it under 150 words. Remove jargon. Keep the billing reference.

Never do this: paste a contract, a case summary, or a client name into a public chatbot. If your firm wants AI on real matter work, use tools with signed data-processing agreements and check your state bar's current guidance first.

Ten prompts every local service business can use today

  1. "Rewrite our Google Business Profile description in 750 characters. Trade: [X]. City: [Y]. Emphasize [differentiator]. No keyword stuffing."
  2. "Turn this job description into a hiring post for [role]. Under 200 words. Mention schedule, pay range placeholder, and one reason people stay here."
  3. "Write a 3-message appointment reminder sequence: 1 week out, 1 day out, 2 hours out. Under 40 words each."
  4. "Draft a polite script for declining a price-shopper who only wants the cheapest quote, while leaving the door open."
  5. "Summarize this 20-minute meeting transcript into decisions, owners, and deadlines: [paste internal notes only]."
  6. "Write a 'what to expect at your first visit' page for a [trade] business. 300 words, reassuring tone."
  7. "Create a 4-week social content calendar for a [trade] in [season]. One post per week, each with a hook, body, and CTA."
  8. "Rewrite this angry customer reply draft to be firm but professional: [paste draft]. Remove anything defensive."
  9. "List 10 questions customers ask before hiring a [trade], ranked by how early they appear in the buying decision."
  10. "Write a 100-word voicemail script for following up on an estimate sent 5 days ago. No pressure, one clear next step."
