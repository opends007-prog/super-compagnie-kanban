EU AI Act Compliance Playbook for SaaS Startups: A Risk-Classification Worksheet You Can Actually Use

The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) entered into force on 1 August 2024. Most provisions apply from 2 August 2026, with earlier dates for prohibited practices (2 February 2025) and general-purpose AI (2 August 2025) [1]. If you ship an AI feature into the EU market — even as a small SaaS — this regulation applies to you. Here is how to classify your system, what obligations follow, and what to build before your next customer call.

Step 1: Determine Whether the Act Applies to You

The Act applies if you:

  • Place an AI system on the EU market (provider), or
  • Deploy an AI system under your name/brand (deployer), or
  • Import or distribute AI systems in the EU.

A US-based SaaS with EU customers is a provider the moment you make the system available to an EU data controller or end user. There is no revenue threshold and no "small company" carve-out from scope — but there is a tiered fine structure and SME-friendly fee reductions [1][2].

Step 2: Classify Your System Into a Risk Tier

The Act uses a four-tier pyramid. Walk through these in order.

Tier 1 — Prohibited (Article 5)

Banned outright. Examples: social scoring by public authorities, untargeted face-scraping for recognition databases, emotion inference in workplaces and schools (with narrow medical/safety exceptions), and predictive policing based solely on profiling [1].

Action: If your feature does any of these, stop. Do not ship.

Tier 2 — High-Risk (Annex III)

These are the systems that trigger the bulk of the obligations. Annex III lists eight categories, including:

  • Employment and worker management (CV screening, promotion decisions)
  • Education and vocational training (admissions, grading)
  • Critical infrastructure (water, gas, electricity, traffic)
  • Access to essential private/public services (credit scoring, insurance pricing, emergency dispatch triage)
  • Law enforcement, migration, and justice
  • Biometric identification and categorisation

If your SaaS is, say, a CV-screening API used by EU recruiters, you are high-risk. If you are a generic LLM-powered customer-support chatbot, you are almost certainly not high-risk under Annex III — but read the GPAI rules below [1][3].

Tier 3 — Limited Risk (Transparency Obligations, Article 50)

You must disclose AI interaction to natural persons. Concretely:

  • Chatbots: tell users they are talking to an AI (unless obvious from context).
  • Synthetic media: label AI-generated images, audio, and video as artificially generated.
  • Emotion recognition or biometric categorisation: inform the exposed person.

A "Summarize this PDF" feature does not trigger Article 50. A "Talk to our AI sales agent" widget does [1].

Tier 4 — Minimal Risk

Everything else. No mandatory obligations beyond general product safety and data protection law (GDPR still applies). Voluntary codes of conduct encouraged.

Step 3: Check the General-Purpose AI (GPAI) Overlay

If you fine-tune, integrate, or substantially modify a GPAI model (e.g., GPT-class, Claude-class, Llama-class), two extra rule sets apply:

  • All GPAI models: technical documentation, training-data summary, copyright compliance, and a public downstream-usage policy [1][4].
  • GPAI with systemic risk (≥10²⁵ FLOPs of training compute, or designated by the Commission): model evaluations, adversarial testing, serious-incident reporting, and cybersecurity protection [1][4].

The Commission has published a non-exhaustive list of systemic-risk models; check it before claiming exemption [4].

Step 5: What Each Tier Actually Requires

High-Risk Path (Annex III systems)

Before placing the system on the market, you need [1][3]:

  1. Risk management system across the full lifecycle (Article 9).
  2. Data governance — training/validation/test data must be relevant, representative, and as error-free as possible (Article 10).
  3. Technical documentation per Annex IV (architecture, data, performance, risk measures).
  4. Logging — automatic record-keeping of events (Article 12).
  5. Transparency — instructions for use covering capabilities, limitations, and intended purpose (Article 13).
  6. Human oversight — designed so that natural persons can intervene, override, or shut down (Article 14).
  7. Accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity — appropriate to intended purpose (Article 15).
  8. Quality management system (Article 17).
  9. Conformity assessment — internal for most Annex III systems; third-party (notified body) for biometric identification (Article 43).
  10. CE marking, EU declaration of conformity, registration in the EU database (Articles 48, 49, 71).
  11. Post-market monitoring and serious-incident reporting within 15 days (Articles 72, 73).

Limited-Risk Path (Article 50)

  • Surface a clear "You are interacting with AI" notice at the start of the conversation.
  • For generated images/audio/video, mark outputs as artificially generated or manipulated in a machine-readable way.
  • Keep a one-page disclosure record per feature for your own audit trail.

GPAI Path

  • Publish a sufficiently detailed training-data summary.
  • Maintain technical documentation for downstream deployers.
  • For systemic-risk models: conduct red-teaming, track serious incidents, and protect model weights.

Step 6: A Realistic 30-Day Plan for a Seed-Stage SaaS

Week 1 — Inventory. List every AI feature in production or in the roadmap. Run each through the worksheet. Tag features with risk tier in your issue tracker.

Week 2 — Gap analysis. For each high-risk feature, map current state against Articles 9–15. The honest output is usually a list of missing artefacts: no Annex IV doc, no logging, no human-override UI.

Week 3 — Documentation sprint. Draft the Annex IV technical doc, the Article 13 instructions-for-use, and the Article 50 disclosure copy. These are mostly writing, not engineering.

Week 4 — Engineering hardening. Add structured logging, a kill-switch or override UI for high-risk features, and an incident-reporting mailbox. Register as a provider in the EU AI database if you have any high-risk or GPAI-with-systemic-risk obligations.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming "we're just a wrapper around an LLM" exempts you. It does not. If you fine-tune or substantially modify, GPAI obligations attach; if your downstream use is high-risk, the deployer obligations attach to your customer and provider obligations may attach to you [1][4].
  • Confusing GDPR with the AI Act. GDPR governs personal data processing. The AI Act governs the system itself. You need both.
  • Treating the Act as a one-time checkbox. High-risk systems require continuous post-market monitoring and incident reporting [1].
  • Skipping the conformity assessment. Self-assessing without the required documentation is the fastest path to a fine.

Penalties (For Context)

  • Prohibited practices: up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher [1][2].
  • Other infringements (including high-risk obligations): up to €15 million or 3%.
  • Supplying incorrect information to authorities: up to €7.5 million or 1%.

SME fee reductions apply to certain administrative fees, not to fines [1][2].
